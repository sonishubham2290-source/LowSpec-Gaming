FPS OPTIMIZATION

How to Increase FPS in Low-End PC Games: 15 Settings That Actually Work

If your PC struggles with modern games, you do not always need new hardware. The right graphics settings can reduce GPU load, improve frame consistency and make a game feel much smoother.

There is no single setting that magically doubles FPS on every computer. The best results come from reducing the settings that are expensive for your particular hardware while keeping the image quality you care about.

Before changing settings: know your PC

Check your CPU, GPU, RAM and available storage space first. Integrated graphics such as Intel UHD or AMD Radeon integrated graphics share system memory, so texture quality and resolution can matter more than they do on a dedicated GPU.

15 settings and fixes to try

1. Lower the game resolution

Resolution is one of the biggest GPU workloads. If 1080p is too demanding, try 1600×900 or 1280×720. A lower resolution can provide a substantial performance improvement, although the image will look softer.

2. Use a sensible resolution scale

If the game has a render scale option, reducing it can lower the internal rendering resolution while keeping the display resolution unchanged. Start with a moderate reduction instead of immediately choosing the lowest value.

3. Reduce shadows

Shadow quality can be expensive, especially in games with large outdoor scenes. Try Low or Medium first. If you need more performance, lowering shadow distance can also help when that option is available.

4. Lower reflections

Screen-space reflections and other reflection effects can add significant GPU work. Set reflections to Low or turn them off if the game allows it.

5. Reduce ambient occlusion

Ambient occlusion adds contact shading around objects. It can improve visual depth, but it is not essential for gameplay. Disable it or use the lowest setting when FPS is limited.

6. Keep textures within your VRAM limit

Textures do not always have the same FPS impact as shadows or resolution, but settings that exceed available video memory can cause stuttering or texture-streaming problems. If your GPU has limited VRAM, use Low or Medium textures.

7. Disable motion blur

Motion blur is mainly a visual preference. Turning it off can make fast camera movement look clearer and removes an effect you may not need on a low-end setup.

8. Use FSR, XeSS or another supported upscaler

If your game supports an upscaling technology, test its Quality or Balanced mode first. Performance modes can give more FPS but may make the image noticeably softer. Availability and results depend on the game and GPU.

9. Lower effects and volumetric quality

Effects such as particles, fog, volumetric lighting and volumetric clouds can be expensive. Lowering these options is often a better compromise than making every setting Low.

10. Reduce view distance when necessary

View distance can increase CPU and GPU workload by requiring the game to process more objects. If a game is CPU-limited, lowering view distance may help more than changing a small visual effect.

11. Close unnecessary background apps

Browsers with many tabs, launchers and other background programs can consume RAM and CPU time. Close applications you do not need before launching a demanding game.

12. Use the correct Windows power setting

On laptops, performance can change depending on the power mode and whether the charger is connected. For gaming, use a performance-oriented power mode when appropriate and keep the laptop connected to its charger if the manufacturer recommends it for full performance.

13. Keep graphics drivers updated

A compatible graphics driver can fix bugs and improve support for games. Use the official driver source for your GPU manufacturer and avoid random “FPS booster” programs.

14. Watch for thermal throttling

If FPS starts high and falls after several minutes, heat can be part of the problem. Check temperatures and make sure vents are not blocked. A clean cooling system and adequate airflow can help a system maintain its normal performance.

15. Find the real bottleneck

If the GPU is near full usage, lowering GPU-heavy settings is usually the logical first step. If the GPU has plenty of headroom while the CPU is heavily loaded, lowering CPU-heavy options such as view distance, crowd density or simulation settings may help more.

Why FPS can still be low after optimization

Optimization has limits. A game may simply require more CPU, GPU or memory than your PC can provide. Some games are also poorly optimized or have heavy areas that cause temporary FPS drops. Good optimization should aim for a smoother and more consistent experience, not promise a specific FPS number for every PC.

Quick checklist

  • Lower resolution or render scale.
  • Lower shadows, reflections and volumetric effects.
  • Keep textures within your VRAM limit.
  • Try Quality/Balanced upscaling.
  • Close unnecessary background programs.
  • Use an appropriate Windows power mode.
  • Update your graphics driver from the official source.
  • Check for thermal throttling and identify the CPU/GPU bottleneck.

Frequently asked questions

Can lowering graphics settings increase FPS?

Yes. Lowering GPU-heavy settings reduces the amount of work the graphics hardware has to do. The improvement depends on which component is limiting performance.

Is 720p good for a low-end PC?

720p can be a practical choice when 1080p is too demanding. It sacrifices image sharpness for lower rendering workload.

Should I use an FPS booster?

Be careful with third-party FPS booster software. Many are unnecessary, and some make questionable changes. Start with the game's own settings and normal Windows configuration first.

Final thoughts

The most useful low-end PC optimization is targeted optimization. Do not automatically set everything to Low. Lower the settings that are actually limiting your system, keep the settings that have little performance cost, and test changes one at a time.
